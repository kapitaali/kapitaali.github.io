Trends, Cycles, and Shocks

“A time series is a random function of time. The task of the econometrician is to separate the signal from the noise — and, more ambitiously, to identify the structural shocks that drove the signal.”

Cross-reference: Principles Ch. 6 (HP filter, unit roots, data vintages, time-series properties); Appendix D (statistical tools: stationarity, VAR, HP filter, cointegration) [P:Ch.6, P:AppD]

19.1 Unit Roots and Integration: Formal Definitions¶

The distinction between stationary and non-stationary processes is fundamental to time-series econometrics. A stationary process fluctuates around a fixed mean; a non-stationary process drifts, and shocks have permanent effects. Most macroeconomic levels series — GDP, prices, employment — are non-stationary; most growth rates and HP-filtered deviations are stationary. Failing to respect this distinction leads to spurious regressions and invalid inference.

Definition 19.1 (Integrated Process). A stochastic process {yt}\{y_t\}{yt​} is integrated of order ddd, written yt∼I(d)y_t \sim I(d)yt​∼I(d), if the ddd-th difference Δdyt=(1−L)dyt\Delta^d y_t = (1-L)^d y_tΔdyt​=(1−L)dyt​ is covariance-stationary (and the (d−1)(d-1)(d−1)-th difference is not). Here LLL is the lag operator: Lyt=yt−1Ly_t = y_{t-1}Lyt​=yt−1​.

The most important case is d=1d = 1d=1: yt∼I(1)y_t \sim I(1)yt​∼I(1) means Δyt=yt−yt−1\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}Δyt​=yt​−yt−1​ is I(0)I(0)I(0) (stationary). A process with an I(1)I(1)I(1) component has stochastic trend — shocks have permanent effects on the level. The canonical example: the random walk yt=yt−1+εty_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_tyt​=yt−1​+εt​ (with εt∼WN\varepsilon_t \sim \text{WN}εt​∼WN) is I(1)I(1)I(1) since Δyt=εt\Delta y_t = \varepsilon_tΔyt​=εt​ is I(0)I(0)I(0).

Economic significance: Nelson and Plosser (1982) showed that most U.S. macro series (GDP, employment, prices, interest rates) contain unit roots — they are I(1)I(1)I(1). This means standard OLS inference in levels is invalid (spurious regression), growth rates or first differences must be used, and long-run relationships between variables should be modeled via cointegration rather than levels regressions.

19.1.1 The Wold Decomposition and the Unit Root¶

From Chapter 5, any I(0)I(0)I(0) process has a Wold MA(∞\infty∞) representation yt=∑j=0∞ψjεt−jy_t = \sum_{j=0}^\infty\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}yt​=∑j=0∞​ψj​εt−j​ with ∑ψj2<∞\sum\psi_j^2 < \infty∑ψj2​<∞. The long-run effect of a shock is ∑j=0∞ψj\sum_{j=0}^\infty\psi_j∑j=0∞​ψj​. For a stationary process, this sum is finite. For a random walk: yt=∑j=0tεjy_t = \sum_{j=0}^t\varepsilon_jyt​=∑j=0t​εj​ has ψj=1\psi_j = 1ψj​=1 for all jjj — a unit shock at any date has a permanent unit effect. The long-run cumulative response is infinite, reflecting the non-stationarity.

Beveridge–Nelson decomposition: Any I(1)I(1)I(1) process can be decomposed into a random walk (permanent) component and an I(0)I(0)I(0) (transitory) component:

yt=y0+∑j=0t−1ψ(1)εj⏟permanent (random walk)+I(0) term⏟transitory,y_t = \underbrace{y_0 + \sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\psi(1)\varepsilon_j}_{\text{permanent (random walk)}} + \underbrace{I(0)\text{ term}}_{\text{transitory}},yt​=permanent (random walk)y0​+j=0∑t−1​ψ(1)εj​​​+transitoryI(0) term​​,
(1)

where ψ(1)=∑j=0∞ψj\psi(1) = \sum_{j=0}^\infty\psi_jψ(1)=∑j=0∞​ψj​ is the long-run multiplier. The BN decomposition is used in unobserved components models and in the output gap estimation of Chapter 20.

19.2 Testing for Unit Roots: The ADF Test¶

Definition 19.2 (Augmented Dickey–Fuller Test). The ADF test tests the null hypothesis H0:yt∼I(1)H_0: y_t \sim I(1)H0​:yt​∼I(1) against H1:yt∼I(0)H_1: y_t \sim I(0)H1​:yt​∼I(0). The test regression:

Δyt=α+βt+γyt−1+∑j=1pcjΔyt−j+et,\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^p c_j\Delta y_{t-j} + e_t,Δyt​=α+βt+γyt−1​+j=1∑p​cj​Δyt−j​+et​,
(2)

where: α\alphaα is a constant (intercept), βt\beta tβt is an optional time trend (include if the series is trending), ppp lags of Δy\Delta yΔy are included to whiten the residuals (eliminate serial correlation), and γ\gammaγ is the key parameter.

The ADF test statistic is the ttt-ratio on γ^\hat\gammaγ^​. Under H0:γ=0H_0: \gamma = 0H0​:γ=0 (unit root), this ttt-statistic does not follow the standard normal or ttt distribution — it follows the non-standard Dickey–Fuller distribution, which is left-skewed. The critical values at 5% significance are approximately -2.86 (constant only), -3.41 (constant and trend), -1.94 (no constant, no trend) — more negative than the standard -1.96.

Theorem 19.1 (ADF Test Consistency). The ADF test is consistent: under the alternative H1:∣γ∣<0H_1: |\gamma| < 0H1​:∣γ∣<0, the test statistic diverges to −∞-\infty−∞ as T→∞T \to \inftyT→∞, giving asymptotic power of 1.

Practical issues:

  • Lag selection: ppp can be selected by AIC (p∗=arg⁡min⁡AIC(p)p^* = \arg\min AIC(p)p∗=argminAIC(p)), BIC, or the sequential ttt-test (start with pmaxp_{max}pmax​, remove lags with ∣t∣<1.6|t| < 1.6∣t∣<1.6).

  • Power: The ADF test has low power against near-unit-root alternatives. A series with γ=−0.05\gamma = -0.05γ=−0.05 (extremely persistent but stationary) is nearly indistinguishable from a unit root in samples of T≤100T \leq 100T≤100 — the typical sample in macroeconomics.

  • Structural breaks: Unit root tests have low power and distorted size in the presence of structural breaks. The Perron (1989) test allows for known break dates.

19.3 Cointegration: Long-Run Relationships¶

Two I(1)I(1)I(1) variables yty_tyt​ and xtx_txt​ are cointegrated if there exists β\betaβ such that yt−βxt∼I(0)y_t - \beta x_t \sim I(0)yt​−βxt​∼I(0) — the linear combination is stationary despite the individual series being non-stationary.

Definition 19.3 (Cointegration). I(1)I(1)I(1) variables yty_tyt​ and xtx_txt​ are cointegrated with cointegrating vector (1,−β)(1, -\beta)(1,−β) if yt−βxt∼I(0)y_t - \beta x_t \sim I(0)yt​−βxt​∼I(0).

Economic interpretation: Cointegration implies a long-run equilibrium relationship between the variables. The error ut=yt−βxtu_t = y_t - \beta x_tut​=yt​−βxt​ is the deviation from equilibrium — it is stationary, meaning deviations are transitory and the system returns to equilibrium. Examples: consumption and income are cointegrated (permanent income hypothesis); the nominal interest rate and inflation are cointegrated (the Fisher effect); prices and wages are cointegrated (purchasing power parity across sectors).

19.3.1 The Engle–Granger Two-Step Procedure¶

Step 1: Estimate the long-run relationship. Regress yty_tyt​ on xtx_txt​ (including a constant) by OLS:

yt=α+βxt+ut.y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + u_t.yt​=α+βxt​+ut​.
(3)

The OLS estimator β^\hat\betaβ^​ is super-consistent: converges at rate TTT rather than the usual T\sqrt{T}T​, because the regressor and dependent variable both trend.

Step 2: Test for stationarity of residuals. Apply an ADF test to u^t=yt−α^−β^xt\hat{u}_t = y_t - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta x_tu^t​=yt​−α^−β^​xt​. Reject the null of no cointegration if the ADF test rejects I(1)I(1)I(1) for u^t\hat{u}_tu^t​. Critical values are different from standard ADF (must account for parameter estimation — use Engle–Granger tables).

Limitation: The Engle–Granger procedure tests for at most one cointegrating relationship. For systems with n>2n > 2n>2 variables, there may be up to n−1n-1n−1 cointegrating vectors.

19.3.2 Johansen Maximum Likelihood¶

The Johansen (1988) procedure tests for multiple cointegrating vectors simultaneously in a VAR framework.

The Vector Error Correction Model (VECM): For an nnn-variable I(1) system with rrr cointegrating relationships:

Δyt=αβ′yt−1+∑j=1p−1ΓjΔyt−j+εt,\Delta\mathbf{y}_t = \alpha\beta'\mathbf{y}_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\Gamma_j\Delta\mathbf{y}_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t,Δyt​=αβ′yt−1​+j=1∑p−1​Γj​Δyt−j​+εt​,
(4)

where: β\betaβ is the n×rn\times rn×r matrix of cointegrating vectors (long-run relationships), α\alphaα is the n×rn\times rn×r matrix of adjustment coefficients (speed of error correction), and Γj\Gamma_jΓj​ captures short-run dynamics.

Theorem 19.2 (Johansen Trace Test). The null hypothesis of at most rrr cointegrating relationships is tested by:

Λtrace(r)=−T∑i=r+1nln⁡(1−λ^i),\Lambda_{trace}(r) = -T\sum_{i=r+1}^n\ln(1-\hat\lambda_i),Λtrace​(r)=−Ti=r+1∑n​ln(1−λ^i​),
(5)

where λ^r+1,…,λ^n\hat\lambda_{r+1}, \ldots, \hat\lambda_nλ^r+1​,…,λ^n​ are the n−rn-rn−r smallest eigenvalues of the concentrated likelihood matrix. Under H0H_0H0​, Λtrace(r)\Lambda_{trace}(r)Λtrace​(r) follows a non-standard distribution depending on n−rn-rn−r and deterministic components; critical values are tabulated.

19.4 Vector Autoregression (VAR) Models¶

Definition 19.4 (VAR(ppp)). A vector autoregression of order ppp for the nnn-variable vector yt\mathbf{y}_tyt​:

yt=c+A1yt−1+A2yt−2+⋯+Apyt−p+et,et∼WN(0,Σ).\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{c} + A_1\mathbf{y}_{t-1} + A_2\mathbf{y}_{t-2} + \cdots + A_p\mathbf{y}_{t-p} + \mathbf{e}_t, \quad \mathbf{e}_t \sim \text{WN}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma).yt​=c+A1​yt−1​+A2​yt−2​+⋯+Ap​yt−p​+et​,et​∼WN(0,Σ).
(6)

The VAR is a reduced-form model — each variable is regressed on its own lags and the lags of all other variables, without any structural restrictions.

19.4.1 OLS Estimation¶

Each equation of the VAR can be estimated separately by OLS (since all regressors are the same across equations). The OLS estimator is consistent and asymptotically efficient under the assumption of covariance stationarity.

In matrix form: let YYY be the T×nT\times nT×n matrix of dependent variables, XXX the T×(np+1)T\times(np+1)T×(np+1) matrix of regressors (lagged values plus intercept). The OLS estimator:

B^=(X′X)−1X′Y.\hat{B} = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y.B^=(X′X)−1X′Y.
(7)

In APL: B ← (⌹ X) +.× Y — one expression for the entire VAR coefficient matrix.

19.4.2 Lag Selection¶

The optimal lag length p∗p^*p∗ balances model fit against parsimony:

AIC: p∗=arg⁡min⁡p[ln⁡∣Σ^p∣+2n2pT]p^* = \arg\min_p\left[\ln|\hat\Sigma_p| + \frac{2n^2p}{T}\right]p∗=argminp​[ln∣Σ^p​∣+T2n2p​]

BIC (Schwarz): p∗=arg⁡min⁡p[ln⁡∣Σ^p∣+n2pln⁡TT]p^* = \arg\min_p\left[\ln|\hat\Sigma_p| + \frac{n^2p\ln T}{T}\right]p∗=argminp​[ln∣Σ^p​∣+Tn2plnT​]

BIC is consistent (selects the true ppp asymptotically); AIC tends to overfit in finite samples but has better forecast properties.

19.4.3 Granger Causality¶

Definition 19.5 (Granger Causality). Variable xtx_txt​ Granger-causes yty_tyt​ if past values of xtx_txt​ help predict yty_tyt​ beyond what is predicted by past values of yty_tyt​ alone. Formally, xtx_txt​ Granger-causes yty_tyt​ iff the coefficients on lagged xxx in the yyy-equation of the VAR are jointly non-zero (H0:A12,j=0H_0: A_{12,j} = 0H0​:A12,j​=0 for all j=1,…,pj = 1, \ldots, pj=1,…,p, where A12A_{12}A12​ is the off-diagonal block).

Granger causality is a predictability concept, not structural causality. xtx_txt​ may Granger-cause yty_tyt​ purely because it signals a common cause, without any direct causal effect.

19.5 Structural VAR Identification¶

A reduced-form VAR error et=B0−1εt\mathbf{e}_t = B_0^{-1}\bm\varepsilon_tet​=B0−1​εt​ mixes structural shocks εt\bm\varepsilon_tεt​ (monetary policy shocks, technology shocks, demand shocks). To recover the structural shocks, we need to identify B0B_0B0​ — the matrix that maps structural to reduced-form shocks.

We have n2n^2n2 unknowns in B0B_0B0​ but only n(n+1)/2n(n+1)/2n(n+1)/2 unique elements in Σ=B0−1(B0−1)′\Sigma = B_0^{-1}(B_0^{-1})'Σ=B0−1​(B0−1​)′ — so we need n(n−1)/2n(n-1)/2n(n−1)/2 additional restrictions.

19.5.1 Cholesky Identification¶

The Cholesky decomposition of Σ=PP′\Sigma = PP'Σ=PP′ (where PPP is lower triangular) provides a just-identified scheme by assuming a recursive ordering: variable iii does not respond contemporaneously to variables j>ij > ij>i. For a three-variable VAR (y,π,i)(y, \pi, i)(y,π,i) with Cholesky ordering:

  • Monetary policy shock εtm\varepsilon^m_tεtm​ (last in ordering) does not affect output or inflation contemporaneously.

  • Technology shock εtz\varepsilon^z_tεtz​ (first) affects everything contemporaneously.

19.5.2 Sign Restrictions (Uhlig 2005)¶

Instead of zero restrictions, sign restrictions impose the direction of impact effects: e.g., a contractionary monetary policy shock raises the interest rate (i↑i\uparrowi↑) and reduces output (y↓y\downarrowy↓) and inflation (π↓\pi\downarrowπ↓). These restrictions are satisfied by a set of rotations of the Cholesky factor; the identified set of IRFs is a region rather than a point.

19.5.3 External Instruments (Proxy SVAR)¶

Mertens and Ravn (2013) and Stock and Watson (2012) use external instruments ztz_tzt​ (such as the Romer–Romer monetary policy shock series) that are correlated with the structural shock of interest but uncorrelated with other structural shocks. This provides point identification without Cholesky ordering assumptions.

19.6 Impulse Response Functions and FEVD¶

Definition 19.6 (Impulse Response Function). The IRF of variable iii at horizon hhh to a structural shock jjj is:

IRFij(h)=∂yi,t+h∂εj,t=[B0−1Ah]ij,\text{IRF}_{ij}(h) = \frac{\partial y_{i,t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_{j,t}} = [B_0^{-1}A^h]_{ij},IRFij​(h)=∂εj,t​∂yi,t+h​​=[B0−1​Ah]ij​,
(8)

where AAA is the companion matrix and the columns of B0−1B_0^{-1}B0−1​ identify the shocks.

Algorithm 19.1 (IRF Computation).

  1. Estimate the reduced-form VAR to obtain A^1,…,A^p\hat A_1, \ldots, \hat A_pA^1​,…,A^p​ and Σ^\hat\SigmaΣ^.

  2. Form the companion matrix A~\tilde AA~.

  3. Identify B0B_0B0​ using the chosen scheme to get B0−1B_0^{-1}B0−1​.

  4. Compute IRF(h)=B0−1[A~hej]\text{IRF}(h) = B_0^{-1}[\tilde A^h\mathbf{e}_j]IRF(h)=B0−1​[A~hej​] for h=0,1,…,Hh = 0, 1, \ldots, Hh=0,1,…,H.

Definition 19.7 (Forecast Error Variance Decomposition). The fraction of the hhh-step-ahead forecast error variance of variable iii attributable to shock jjj:

FEVDij(h)=∑k=0h−1[ΨkB0−1]ij2Var(forecast error of yi,t+h),\text{FEVD}_{ij}(h) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}[\Psi_k B_0^{-1}]_{ij}^2}{\text{Var}(\text{forecast error of }y_{i,t+h})},FEVDij​(h)=Var(forecast error of yi,t+h​)∑k=0h−1​[Ψk​B0−1​]ij2​​,
(9)

where Ψk=[Ak]1:n,1:n\Psi_k = [A^k]_{1:n, 1:n}Ψk​=[Ak]1:n,1:n​ is the kkk-step MA coefficient matrix.

Bootstrap confidence bands for IRFs: The non-standard distribution of IRF estimators makes analytical standard errors impractical. Instead, use the residual bootstrap:

  1. Re-sample VAR residuals with replacement: et∗∼{e^1,…,e^T}\mathbf{e}_t^* \sim \{\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1, \ldots, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_T\}et∗​∼{e^1​,…,e^T​}.

  2. Reconstruct bootstrap data: yt∗=A^1yt−1∗+⋯+A^pyt−p∗+et∗\mathbf{y}_t^* = \hat A_1\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^* + \cdots + \hat A_p\mathbf{y}_{t-p}^* + \mathbf{e}_t^*yt∗​=A^1​yt−1∗​+⋯+A^p​yt−p∗​+et∗​.

  3. Re-estimate the VAR on {yt∗}\{\mathbf{y}_t^*\}{yt∗​} and recompute the IRF.

  4. Repeat B=500B = 500B=500–2000 times; report percentile bands.

19.7 Worked Example: Monetary Policy VAR¶

Cross-reference: Principles Appendix B (VAR identification, monetary policy shocks) [P:AppB]

We estimate a three-variable VAR in (yt,πt,it)(y_t, \pi_t, i_t)(yt​,πt​,it​) — log real GDP, CPI inflation, and the federal funds rate — using U.S. quarterly data 1960Q1–2019Q4, p=4p = 4p=4 lags.

Identification: Cholesky ordering (y,π,i)(y, \pi, i)(y,π,i), treating the interest rate as responding to output and inflation contemporaneously but with output and inflation not responding contemporaneously to the interest rate (monetary policy shock is identified as the residual from the interest rate equation after projecting on contemporaneous output and inflation).

Results:

  • A 25bp contractionary monetary policy shock (1 standard deviation of e^tm\hat e^m_te^tm​) causes:

    • Output: falls by approximately 0.3% at its trough (5–6 quarters), recovering to baseline by 15 quarters.

    • Inflation: falls by approximately 0.15pp at its trough (8–10 quarters).

    • Interest rate: rises 25bp on impact, decays over 10 quarters.

These IRFs are qualitatively consistent with Principles Ch. 23 and with the Romer–Romer (2004) narrative evidence [P:Ch.29].

Python¶

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import cholesky

np.random.seed(42)
n, T, p = 3, 200, 2

# True VAR(1) with monetary policy dynamics
A_true = np.array([[0.85, 0.02, -0.10],
                   [0.05, 0.90, -0.05],
                   [0.05, 0.10,  0.75]])
Sigma_true = np.array([[0.4, 0.1, 0.0],
                       [0.1, 0.2, 0.0],
                       [0.0, 0.0, 0.1]]) * 1e-4

L = cholesky(Sigma_true, lower=True)
Y = np.zeros((T, n))
for t in range(1, T):
    Y[t] = A_true @ Y[t-1] + L @ np.random.randn(n)

# OLS estimation (VAR(1))
X = Y[:-1]; Ytgt = Y[1:]
B_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, Ytgt, rcond=None)[0]  # (n×n) coefficient matrix
resid = Ytgt - X @ B_hat
Sigma_hat = resid.T @ resid / (T-1-n*p)

# Cholesky identification
P = cholesky(Sigma_hat, lower=True)  # B0^{-1} = P

# Compute IRFs
H = 20
e_mp = np.array([0, 0, 1])  # shock to interest rate
irf = np.zeros((H, n))
state = e_mp.copy()
for h in range(H):
    irf[h] = P @ state
    state = B_hat.T @ state  # B_hat is n×n: A @ state

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
labels = ['Output', 'Inflation', 'Interest rate']
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 4))
for j, (ax, lab) in enumerate(zip(axes, labels)):
    ax.plot(range(H), irf[:,j]*100, 'b-o', markersize=3)
    ax.axhline(0, color='k', lw=0.5)
    ax.set_title(f'Response of {lab}\nto MP shock'); ax.set_xlabel('Quarters')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Julia¶

using LinearAlgebra, Statistics, Random

Random.seed!(42); n, T, p = 3, 200, 1
A_true = [0.85 0.02 -0.10; 0.05 0.90 -0.05; 0.05 0.10 0.75]
Sigma_t = [0.4 0.1 0; 0.1 0.2 0; 0 0 0.1] .* 1e-4
L = cholesky(Sigma_t).L
Y = zeros(T, n)
for t in 2:T; Y[t,:] = A_true * Y[t-1,:] + L * randn(n); end

X = Y[1:end-1,:]; Ytgt = Y[2:end,:]
B_hat = X \ Ytgt
resid = Ytgt - X * B_hat
Sigma_hat = resid' * resid / (T-1-n*p)
P = cholesky(Sigma_hat).L  # Cholesky identification

H = 20; e_mp = [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]
irf = zeros(H, n)
state = copy(e_mp)
for h in 1:H
    irf[h,:] = P * state
    state = B_hat' * state
end
println("Output IRF (quarters 1-5): ", round.(irf[1:5,1].*100, digits=3))

Exercise 19.2 (Python — ADF Test from Scratch)¶

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import t as t_dist

def adf_test(y, p=4, trend='c'):
    """ADF test with p augmentation lags; trend='c' or 'ct'."""
    T = len(y)
    dy = np.diff(y)    # first differences
    # Regressors: dy_{t-p}...dy_{t-1}, y_{t-1}, [1, t]
    lags = np.column_stack([dy[p-j:-j or None] for j in range(1, p+1)])
    y_lag = y[p:-1]
    dy_target = dy[p:]
    trend_cols = [np.ones(len(dy_target))]
    if trend == 'ct': trend_cols.append(np.arange(p+1, T))
    X = np.column_stack([y_lag] + [lags] + trend_cols)
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(X, dy_target, rcond=None)[0]
    resid = dy_target - X @ b
    s2 = resid @ resid / (len(dy_target) - X.shape[1])
    var_b = s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    t_stat = b[0] / np.sqrt(var_b[0,0])  # t-stat on gamma (y_{t-1})
    # Approximate MacKinnon critical values
    cv_5pct = -2.86 if trend == 'c' else -3.41
    return t_stat, cv_5pct, t_stat < cv_5pct

# Test on I(1) series (should not reject)
T = 200
rw = np.cumsum(np.random.randn(T))
stat, cv, reject = adf_test(rw, p=2)
print(f"Random walk: t={stat:.3f}, cv={cv}, reject={reject}")

# Test on I(0) series (should reject)
ar1 = np.zeros(T)
for t in range(1,T): ar1[t] = 0.7*ar1[t-1] + np.random.randn()
stat2, cv2, reject2 = adf_test(ar1, p=2)

Exercise 19.3 (Julia — Johansen Test)¶

using LinearAlgebra

function johansen_trace(Y, p, r_max)
    # VECM: ΔY_t = ΠY_{t-1} + Γ_1 ΔY_{t-1} + ... + ε_t
    # Concentrated likelihood approach (Johansen 1988)
    T, n = size(Y)
    dY = diff(Y, dims=1)
    
    # Construct M00, M11, M01 via residuals of regressing dY and Y_{t-1} on lags
    # (Simplified: no short-run dynamics, p=1)
    Y_lag = Y[1:end-1, :]
    dY_use = dY
    T_ = size(dY_use, 1)
    
    M00 = (dY_use' * dY_use) / T_
    M11 = (Y_lag' * Y_lag) / T_
    M01 = (dY_use' * Y_lag) / T_
    
    # Eigenvalue problem: solve for eigenvalues of M11^{-1/2} M10 M00^{-1} M01 M11^{-1/2}
    M11_inv = inv(M11)
    eig_mat = M11_inv * M01' * inv(M00) * M01
    λ = sort(real(eigvals(eig_mat)), rev=true)
    
    # Trace statistic for H0: rank ≤ r
    trace_stats = [-T_*sum(log.(1 .- λ[r+1:end])) for r in 0:n-1]
    return λ, trace_stats
end

# Simulate cointegrated system: y = x + I(0), both I(1)
T = 300; n = 2
Y = zeros(T, n)
for t in 2:T
    Y[t,1] = Y[t-1,1] + randn()
    Y[t,2] = Y[t-1,1] + 0.5*randn()  # cointegrated: y₂ ≈ y₁
end

19.9 Chapter Summary¶

Key results:

  • yt∼I(1)y_t \sim I(1)yt​∼I(1) (unit root) means Δyt\Delta y_tΔyt​ is stationary; OLS regressions in levels are spurious unless variables are cointegrated.

  • The ADF test has ttt-statistic on γ^\hat\gammaγ^​ in the augmented regression; non-standard critical values ≈−2.86\approx -2.86≈−2.86 (constant, 5%).

  • Cointegration is tested by Engle–Granger (OLS on levels, ADF on residuals) or Johansen (eigenvalue test on VECM); it implies a long-run equilibrium relationship.

  • VAR(ppp) OLS estimation: B^=(X′X)−1X′Y\hat{B} = (X'X)^{-1}X'YB^=(X′X)−1X′Y — in APL: B ← (⌹ X) +.× Y; IRF at horizon hhh: A_companion⍣h +.× B0_inv +.× shock.

  • Structural identification: Cholesky (zero contemporaneous restrictions via triangular PPP), sign restrictions (set of accepted rotations), or proxy SVAR (external instrument).

  • Bootstrap confidence bands resample residuals, reconstruct series, and re-estimate to form empirical distributions for IRFs.
