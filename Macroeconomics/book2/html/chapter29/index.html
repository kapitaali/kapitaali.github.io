Higher-Order Approximations for Nonlinear Dynamics

“The first-order approximation tells you the average. The second-order approximation tells you about the risk. You need both.”

Cross-reference: Principles Ch. 39 (future methods: non-Gaussian risks, rare disasters); Ch. 12 (investment under uncertainty, real options) [P:Ch.39, P:Ch.12]

29.1 Why Go Beyond First Order?¶

Chapter 27’s log-linearization is accurate to O(ε2)O(\varepsilon^2)O(ε2) and is the workhorse for most policy questions. But three economically important phenomena are invisible at first order:

1. Precautionary saving. The Euler equation under uncertainty gives Et[u′(ct+1)]=u′(ct)/β(1+r)\mathbb{E}_t[u'(c_{t+1})] = u'(c_t)/\beta(1+r)Et​[u′(ct+1​)]=u′(ct​)/β(1+r). By Jensen’s inequality, E[u′(c)]>u′(E[c])\mathbb{E}[u'(c)] > u'(\mathbb{E}[c])E[u′(c)]>u′(E[c]) when u′′<0u'' < 0u′′<0 (concavity) and u′′′>0u''' > 0u′′′>0 (prudence, positive third derivative). Households save more than certainty-equivalent income — the precautionary saving motive. This is a second-order effect (∝σc2\propto \sigma_c^2∝σc2​) invisible in the first-order approximation.

2. Risk premia. The equity premium is E[Rt+1e−Rf]=−Cov(Mt+1,Rt+1e)/E[Mt+1]\mathbb{E}[R_{t+1}^e - R^f] = -\text{Cov}(M_{t+1}, R_{t+1}^e) / \mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}]E[Rt+1e​−Rf]=−Cov(Mt+1​,Rt+1e​)/E[Mt+1​]. The covariance between the SDF Mt+1M_{t+1}Mt+1​ and equity returns is a second-order quantity — the product of two first-order deviations. Asset pricing requires at least second-order approximations.

3. ELB dynamics and welfare comparisons. The welfare loss from inflation volatility is L≈κ2Var(π^)+λx2Var(x^)\mathcal{L} \approx \frac{\kappa}{2}\text{Var}(\hat\pi) + \frac{\lambda_x}{2}\text{Var}(\hat{x})L≈2κ​Var(π^)+2λx​​Var(x^). Variances are second-order terms. Comparing welfare across policy regimes requires second-order accuracy.

29.2 The Perturbation Approach¶

Definition 29.1 (Perturbation Parameter). Perturbation methods introduce a scalar ε∈[0,1]\varepsilon \in [0,1]ε∈[0,1] that scales all shocks: the model’s stochastic equations replace εt→ε εt\varepsilon_t \to \varepsilon\,\varepsilon_tεt​→εεt​. When ε=0\varepsilon = 0ε=0: the deterministic steady state. When ε=1\varepsilon = 1ε=1: the full stochastic model. The solution is expanded as a power series in ε\varepsilonε and the state deviations.

The kkk-th order perturbation approximates the policy functions:

c(kt,At;ε)=cSS+ck(kt−k∗)+cA(At−A∗)+12ckk(kt−k∗)2+ckA(kt−k∗)(At−A∗)+12cAAσ2+…c(k_t, A_t; \varepsilon) = c^{SS} + c_k(k_t - k^*) + c_A(A_t - A^*) + \frac{1}{2}c_{kk}(k_t-k^*)^2 + c_{kA}(k_t-k^*)(A_t-A^*) + \frac{1}{2}c_{AA}\sigma^2 + \ldotsc(kt​,At​;ε)=cSS+ck​(kt​−k∗)+cA​(At​−A∗)+21​ckk​(kt​−k∗)2+ckA​(kt​−k∗)(At​−A∗)+21​cAA​σ2+…
(1)

The 12cAAσ2\frac{1}{2}c_{AA}\sigma^221​cAA​σ2 term is the precautionary term — nonzero only at second order and proportional to the variance of shocks.

29.3 Setting Up the Second-Order System¶

For the scalar RBC model with state (kt,At)(k_t, A_t)(kt​,At​) and control ctc_tct​, the equilibrium conditions are:

F(kt+1,ct+1,kt,ct,At+1,At;ε)=0,F(k_{t+1}, c_{t+1}, k_t, c_t, A_{t+1}, A_t; \varepsilon) = 0,F(kt+1​,ct+1​,kt​,ct​,At+1​,At​;ε)=0,

where FFF collects the Euler equation, resource constraint, and TFP process. The solution at order ε0\varepsilon^0ε0: the steady state (k∗,c∗,A∗)(k^*, c^*, A^*)(k∗,c∗,A∗). The solution at order ε1\varepsilon^1ε1: the log-linearized policy functions from Chapter 27. The solution at order ε2\varepsilon^2ε2: the second-order correction.

The second-order expansion. Writing the policy function as:

ct=g(kt,At;ε)=g0+gxxt+12xt′Gxxxt+12gεεε2+εgxεxt+O(ε3),c_t = g(k_t, A_t; \varepsilon) = g_0 + g_x\mathbf{x}_t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}_t'G_{xx}\mathbf{x}_t + \frac{1}{2}g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon g_{x\varepsilon}\mathbf{x}_t + O(\varepsilon^3),ct​=g(kt​,At​;ε)=g0​+gx​xt​+21​xt′​Gxx​xt​+21​gεε​ε2+εgxε​xt​+O(ε3),
(3)

where xt=(kt−k∗,At−A∗)′\mathbf{x}_t = (k_t - k^*, A_t - A^*)'xt​=(kt​−k∗,At​−A∗)′ is the state deviation vector. The new terms at second order:

  • xt′Gxxxt/2\mathbf{x}_t'G_{xx}\mathbf{x}_t/2xt′​Gxx​xt​/2: interaction and squared terms (state-dependent risk adjustment).

  • gεεε2/2g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\varepsilon^2/2gεε​ε2/2: stochastic steady state correction — the constant shift in the level of the policy function due to the presence of risk.

Theorem 29.1 (Breakdown of Certainty Equivalence at Second Order). At first order, the policy function takes the form ct(1)=cSS+gx(1)xtc_t^{(1)} = c^{SS} + g_x^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_tct(1)​=cSS+gx(1)​xt​ — independent of the variance ε2σ2\varepsilon^2\sigma^2ε2σ2. At second order, the stochastic correction gεεε2/2g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\varepsilon^2/2gεε​ε2/2 appears: the policy function depends on the variance of shocks. This is the breakdown of certainty equivalence: agents behave differently under uncertainty than under certainty, even near the steady state.

Proof. At first order: E[Fc]=Fc(E[c])\mathbb{E}[F_c] = F_c(\mathbb{E}[c])E[Fc​]=Fc​(E[c]) (by linearity). The solution depends only on E[xt+1∣xt]=Axt\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t+1}|\mathbf{x}_t] = A\mathbf{x}_tE[xt+1​∣xt​]=Axt​ and does not involve Var[xt+1∣xt]=ε2Q\text{Var}[\mathbf{x}_{t+1}|\mathbf{x}_t] = \varepsilon^2QVar[xt+1​∣xt​]=ε2Q. At second order: the Euler equation involves Et[Fcc(ct+1−c∗)2]\mathbb{E}_t[F_{cc}(c_{t+1}-c^*)^2]Et​[Fcc​(ct+1​−c∗)2] — the variance of future consumption appears. The coefficient gεεg_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}gεε​ is determined by solving the second-order system, which depends on Var[εt+1]\text{Var}[\varepsilon_{t+1}]Var[εt+1​]. □\square□

29.4 Computing the Second-Order Solution¶

The second-order coefficient matrices GxxG_{xx}Gxx​ and gεεg_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}gεε​ are found by differentiating the equilibrium conditions twice with respect to states and the perturbation parameter.

Taking the second derivative of Et[F(⋅;ε)]=0\mathbb{E}_t[F(\cdot;\varepsilon)] = 0Et​[F(⋅;ε)]=0 with respect to (xt,ε)(\mathbf{x}_t, \varepsilon)(xt​,ε) at (x∗,0)(\mathbf{x}^*, 0)(x∗,0):

(4)

where Ay′y′A_{y'y'}Ay′y′​, By′y′B_{y'y'}By′y′​, CyyC_{yy}Cyy​ are collections of second partial derivatives of FFF evaluated at the steady state — computable analytically or by automatic differentiation (ForwardDiff.jl, JAX).

29.5 Pruning: Avoiding Explosive Paths¶

A practical problem with higher-order perturbation: the second-order terms (kt−k∗)2(k_t - k^*)^2(kt​−k∗)2 can grow without bound even when the linear part is stable, because squared deviations can be explosive even if deviations themselves are stable. Pruning (Kim, Kim, Schaumburg, and Sims, 2008) addresses this by separating the first- and second-order components.

Definition 29.2 (Pruning). The pruned second-order approximation maintains two separate state vectors:

xt(1)=Axt−1(1)+Bεt(first-order component),\mathbf{x}_t^{(1)} = A\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{(1)} + B\varepsilon_t \quad \text{(first-order component)},xt(1)​=Axt−1(1)​+Bεt​(first-order component),
(6)
xt(2)=Axt−1(2)+12xt−1(1)′Gxxxt−1(1)+12gεεσ2(second-order correction).\mathbf{x}_t^{(2)} = A\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{(2)} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{(1)'} G_{xx}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{(1)} + \frac{1}{2}g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\sigma^2 \quad \text{(second-order correction)}.xt(2)​=Axt−1(2)​+21​xt−1(1)′​Gxx​xt−1(1)​+21​gεε​σ2(second-order correction).
(7)

The pruned approximation: ct≈cSS+gxxt(1)+xt(1)′Gcxxt(1)/2+gεεσ2/2c_t \approx c^{SS} + g_x\mathbf{x}_t^{(1)} + \mathbf{x}_t^{(1)'}G_{cx}\mathbf{x}_t^{(1)}/2 + g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\sigma^2/2ct​≈cSS+gx​xt(1)​+xt(1)′​Gcx​xt(1)​/2+gεε​σ2/2.

Why pruning works: By separately tracking the first-order state, we prevent the second-order correction from feeding back into the first-order dynamics. The first-order component is stable (by the Blanchard–Kahn conditions); the second-order correction inherits this stability because it is driven by the square of the stable first-order component, which remains bounded.

29.6 Worked Example: Precautionary Saving in the RBC Model¶

Cross-reference: Principles Ch. 11.5 (buffer-stock saving, precautionary motive) [P:Ch.11.5]

Setup: Standard RBC calibration (α=0.36\alpha = 0.36α=0.36, δ=0.025\delta = 0.025δ=0.025, β=0.99\beta = 0.99β=0.99, σ=2\sigma = 2σ=2, ρA=0.95\rho_A = 0.95ρA​=0.95, σA=0.0072\sigma_A = 0.0072σA​=0.0072).

First-order solution: At first order, the consumption policy function is c^t=gk(1)k^t+gA(1)A^t\hat{c}_t = g_k^{(1)}\hat{k}_t + g_A^{(1)}\hat{A}_tc^t​=gk(1)​k^t​+gA(1)​A^t​. The stochastic steady state coincides with the deterministic steady state: E[c^]=0\mathbb{E}[\hat{c}] = 0E[c^]=0.

Second-order correction: The precautionary saving term shifts the stochastic steady state below the deterministic one: c^SS,2=gεεσA2/2<0\hat{c}^{SS,2} = g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\sigma_A^2/2 < 0c^SS,2=gεε​σA2​/2<0. Households facing income uncertainty save more than under certainty — they hold a buffer stock.

Quantitative magnitude: For the standard calibration, gεεσA2/2≈−0.5σA2⋅σ=−0.5×(0.0072)2×2≈−5×10−5g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\sigma_A^2/2 \approx -0.5\sigma_A^2\cdot\sigma = -0.5\times(0.0072)^2\times 2 \approx -5\times10^{-5}gεε​σA2​/2≈−0.5σA2​⋅σ=−0.5×(0.0072)2×2≈−5×10−5, i.e., approximately 0.005% of steady-state consumption. Small but nonzero — and critical for welfare analysis.

Python¶

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_lyapunov

# RBC model: first-order solution and precautionary saving computation
alpha, delta, beta, sigma_crra = 0.36, 0.025, 0.99, 2.0
rho_A, sig_A = 0.95, 0.0072

# Steady state
r_ss = 1/beta - 1 + delta
k_ss = (alpha/r_ss)**(1/(1-alpha))
c_ss = k_ss**alpha - delta*k_ss
y_ss = k_ss**alpha

print(f"Steady state: k*={k_ss:.4f}, c*={c_ss:.4f}, y*={y_ss:.4f}")

# First-order policy functions (from log-linearization and gensys):
# From solving the linearized RBC, coefficients approx:
g_k = (1 - delta*(1 - alpha)) / (1 - beta*(1-delta))  # rough estimate
g_A = 1.0 / (1 - alpha*beta*rho_A)  # output response to TFP

print(f"\nFirst-order policy: ĉ_t ≈ {g_k:.3f}*k̂_t + {g_A:.3f}*Â_t")

# Second-order stochastic correction:
# From Euler eq: E[u'(c_{t+1})] = u'(c^SS) / [beta*(1+r)]
# Under uncertainty: E[(c^SS + hat_c)^{-sigma}] ≈ (c^SS)^{-sigma}(1 + sigma*(sigma+1)/2 * Var(hat_c)/c^SS^2)
# Stochastic steady state: hat_c^SS_2 = -sigma/2 * Var(hat_c) / c^SS^2 * (something)

# Variance of consumption: from Lyapunov equation on linearized system
# State: hat_k
# hat_k_{t+1} = (1-delta)*hat_k_t + delta/y_ss * g_k * hat_k_t + delta*g_A*hat_A_t + ... 
# Approximate: Var(hat_c) = g_A^2 * Var(hat_A)
var_A = sig_A**2 / (1 - rho_A**2)
var_c1 = g_A**2 * var_A  # rough first-order variance

# Precautionary saving term (second-order stochastic SS shift)
# From Kimball prudence: savings increase proportional to var(c)*prudence
prudence = sigma_crra * (sigma_crra + 1)  # CRRA: u'''(c) > 0
precautionary_correction = -0.5 * prudence * var_c1  # approximate
print(f"\nPrecautionary saving term: {precautionary_correction:.6f} (≈ {100*precautionary_correction:.4f}% of SS consumption)")
print(f"Var(ĉ) ≈ {var_c1:.6f}, std(ĉ) ≈ {np.sqrt(var_c1)*100:.2f}%")

# Compare 1st vs 2nd order moments
print(f"\nFirst order: E[ĉ] = 0 (no precautionary correction)")
print(f"Second order: E[ĉ] ≈ {precautionary_correction:.6f} (precautionary motive)")

# For illustration: compare IRF shapes qualitatively
alpha, delta, beta, rho_A, sig_A = 0.36, 0.025, 0.99, 0.95, 0.0072
sigma_crra = 2.0

H = 20
irf_1st = zeros(H); irf_2nd = zeros(H)

# First-order IRF: exponential decay
for h in 1:H
    irf_1st[h] = rho_A^(h-1) * sig_A
end

# Second-order correction: includes (shock)^2 term
# Rough approximation: adds concavity correction
for h in 1:H
    base = rho_A^(h-1)
    corr = -0.5 * sigma_crra * base^2 * sig_A^2  # second-order concavity term
    irf_2nd[h] = base * sig_A + corr
end

println("TFP IRF comparison (1st vs 2nd order, first 5 periods):")
for h in 1:5
    println("  h=$h: 1st-order=$(round(irf_1st[h],digits=6)), 2nd-order=$(round(irf_2nd[h],digits=6)), diff=$(round(irf_2nd[h]-irf_1st[h],digits=8))")
end
println("  (2nd-order correction is O(σ²) ≈ $(round(sig_A^2,digits=8)) per period)")

29.8 Chapter Summary¶

Key results:

  • First-order perturbation satisfies certainty equivalence — the stochastic solution equals the deterministic one. Second-order perturbation breaks this: the stochastic steady state is shifted by a term proportional to σ2\sigma^2σ2 (shock variance).

  • The second-order policy function has the form ct=cSS+gxxt+12xt′Gxxxt+12gεεσ2c_t = c^{SS} + g_x\mathbf{x}_t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}_t'G_{xx}\mathbf{x}_t + \frac{1}{2}g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\sigma^2ct​=cSS+gx​xt​+21​xt′​Gxx​xt​+21​gεε​σ2, with a precautionary saving term gεεσ2/2<0g_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\sigma^2/2 < 0gεε​σ2/2<0 for CRRA households.

  • The second-order coefficient matrices GxxG_{xx}Gxx​ are computed by solving a linear system involving second derivatives of equilibrium conditions (via ForwardDiff or analytical expressions).

  • Pruning (Kim et al., 2008) separates first- and second-order state components to prevent explosive paths while retaining second-order accuracy.

  • Risk premia and welfare comparisons across policy regimes require second-order accuracy; first-order models give equity premia of exactly zero and cannot rank policies by welfare.

  • In Dynare: order=2 and order=3 activate higher-order perturbation; pruning option implements the Kim et al. scheme.
